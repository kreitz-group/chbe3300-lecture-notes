After completing this chapter, you should be able to:

- Distinguish open, closed, and isolated systems, and identify which apply to common chemical
  reactors.
- Apply the general balance equation to total mass, individual species, and total moles, and
  identify when the production term vanishes.
- Express the species production term in a homogeneous reacting volume in terms of stoichiometric
  coefficients, reaction rates, and the volume.
- Derive the molar and concentration balances for an ideal batch reactor at constant volume,
  including the multi-reaction matrix form.
- Modify the concentration balance when the reactor volume changes during reaction.

Having discussed stoichiometry and thermodynamics, we now switch gears to chemical reactors. So far
we have not asked *where* our reactions are happening: in the $\ce{NH3}$-synthesis example the word
*system* has been used loosely. We now define the types of systems and develop a general balance
equation that applies to all of them. In CHBE 3300 we do not cover energy balances, and we restrict
ourselves to isothermal processes throughout. Mass balances are the foundation for everything that
follows in reaction kinetics.

## Types of systems

:::{admonition} Discussion
:class: seealso
In the beginning, there was a system. Three categories cover everything. What are the three types
of systems, and how do they differ in what they exchange with their surroundings?
:::

The three categories are *open*, *closed*, and *isolated* systems, shown in [](#fig-systems). An
open system can exchange both mass and energy with its surroundings; a closed system can exchange
energy but not mass; and an isolated system exchanges neither.

:::{figure} ../figures/Systems.png
:label: fig-systems
:alt: Three hand-drawn panels comparing system types, each showing a reactor box in which A reacts to B, enclosed by a dashed system boundary that separates it from the surroundings. Left, isolated system: nothing crosses the boundary. Middle, closed system: a heat flow arrow Q crosses the boundary but no mass does. Right, open system: mass flow arrows enter on the left and leave on the right, and a heat flow arrow Q also crosses. A table below compares them. Energy exchange: no, yes, yes. Mass exchange: no, no, yes. Reactions: limited, limited, yes. The corresponding real systems are given as none in practice apart from an adiabatic batch reactor or the universe; a batch reactor; and a CSTR or plug-flow reactor.
:width: 95%

Types of systems.
:::

Real chemical reactors are typically open or closed systems; truly isolated reactors are an
idealization rarely encountered in practice.

In order to describe and design reactors, we need balance equations. Some of you probably know this
already, but for any system we can define the **general balance equation** as

$$
\text{accumulation} = \text{in} - \text{out}
+ \underbrace{\text{generation} - \text{consumption}}_{\text{production term}} .
$$ (eq-gen-balance)

We can apply this balance equation to all sorts of quantities:

- mass
- momentum
- molar amount
- energy
- atoms

Formally,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\mathrm{d}t} = \dot{z}_0 - \dot{z} + \dot{p} ,
$$ (eq-gen-balance-rate)

where $z$ stands for any extensive quantity — mass $m$, molar amount $n$, or energy $E$, for
instance — and the dotted symbols denote the corresponding flow and production rates.

## Material balances

To develop the mass balance of a reactor, consider a species $A_i$ inside a fixed region of space,
the **control volume**, bounded by the system boundary. The species can cross the boundary on entry
or exit, and inside the control volume it can be consumed or generated by chemical reaction.

### a) Mass balance

:::{figure} ../figures/MassBalance.png
:label: fig-mass-balance
:alt: A rectangular control volume holding mass m of species i, with the total mass written as the sum over all species. One arrow enters from the left carrying the inlet mass flow rate of species i, and one arrow leaves to the right carrying the outlet mass flow rate of species i. Annotations below state that the total inlet mass flow rate is the sum of the species inlet flow rates, and likewise for the outlet.
:width: 60%

Sketch for the mass balance of the system.
:::

For the overall mass balance,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}t} = \dot{m}_0 - \dot{m} \qquad (\dot{p} = 0) .
$$ (eq-mass-total)

The overall mass is conserved: we neither create nor destroy mass, since we are not considering
nuclear reactions. The dotted quantities are mass flow rates, with units of kg s$^{-1}$. There is
no production term because mass is conserved.

For individual components, however,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}m_i}{\mathrm{d}t} = \dot{m}_{i,0} - \dot{m}_i + \dot{p}_i ,
$$ (eq-mass-species)

since a species can be transformed in a chemical reaction.

### b) Mole balance

:::{figure} ../figures/MoleBalance.png
:label: fig-mole-balance
:alt: The same control volume as the previous sketch, but counted in moles rather than mass. The box holds the moles of species i, with the total moles written as the sum over all species. One arrow enters from the left carrying the inlet molar flow rate of species i, and one arrow leaves to the right carrying the outlet molar flow rate. Annotations below state that the total inlet molar flow rate is the sum of the species inlet flow rates, and likewise for the outlet.
:width: 60%

Sketch for the mole balance of the system.
:::

For the overall number of moles,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}t} = \dot{n}_0 - \dot{n} + \dot{p} ,
$$ (eq-mole-total)

where $\dot{n}$ and $\dot{n}_0$ are molar flow rates with units of mol s$^{-1}$. Here the overall
production term can be nonzero, $\dot{p} \neq 0$ — for the ammonia synthesis
$\ce{N2 + 3 H2 <=> 2 NH3}$, for example, the number of moles changes from 4 to 2. For the
individual components,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}n_i}{\mathrm{d}t} = \dot{n}_{i,0} - \dot{n}_i + \dot{p}_i .
$$ (eq-mole-species)

You can of course convert one balance into the other using $m_i = n_i M_i$, where $M_i$ is the
molar mass of species $i$. **We will typically work with the mole balance in this course.**

The production or generation term is of particular importance to us, because it is where the
chemical reactions enter. Its general definition for a species $i$ in a volume, assuming a single
chemical reaction, is

$$
\dot{p}_i = \int_0^V \nu_i r \, \mathrm{d}V .
$$ (eq-prod-integral)

In general, $r$ depends on position within the volume.

:::{admonition} Discussion
:class: seealso
What is the sign of $\nu_i$ if species $i$ is a product of the reaction? What if it is a reactant?
:::

If the system is homogeneous, $r$ is uniform throughout the volume and the integral collapses to

$$
\dot{p}_i = \nu_i r V ,
$$ (eq-prod-homog)

and for $M$ simultaneous reactions,

$$
\dot{p}_i = V \sum_{j=1}^{M} \nu_{ij} r_j .
$$ (eq-prod-homog-multi)

We can now also determine the units of the reaction rate $r$:

$$
r\ [=]\ \frac{\text{moles}}{\text{volume} \cdot \text{time}} .
$$

Note the time *and* volume dependence of the reaction rate. Substituting leads to

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}n_i}{\mathrm{d}t} = \dot{n}_{i,0} - \dot{n}_i + \int_0^V \nu_i r \, \mathrm{d}V .
$$

## Material balance of the batch reactor

There are three general reactor types, classified by their mode of operation and type of mixing:
the **batch reactor**, the **continuously stirred tank reactor (CSTR)**, and the **plug-flow
reactor (PFR)**. We focus on the batch reactor in this course as the simplest of the three,
although operating one in practice is still nontrivial.

:::{figure} ../figures/BatchReactor.png
:label: fig-batch-reactor
:alt: Left, a sketch of a real stirred batch reactor: a round-bottomed vessel filled to a marked liquid level, with a stirrer on a central shaft, a probe at the top measuring pressure and temperature, and a surrounding jacket labelled heating and cooling. An arrow points right to the idealization used in this course: a plain rectangular vessel with the same liquid level and stirrer, and no jacket or probe. Two assumptions are listed alongside it, isothermal and ideally mixed.
:width: 85%

Batch reactor, one phase / homogeneous.
:::

Batch reactors are used in

- small-scale operations, including labs
- pharmaceuticals
- semiconductor processing
- research
- biotechnology

Let us formulate the mass balance for our batch reactor. With no flow in and no flow out,
$\dot{n}_0 = \dot{n} = 0$, which simplifies the molar balance considerably:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d}n_i}{\mathrm{d}t} &= \dot{n}_{i,0} - \dot{n}_i + \int_0^V \nu_i r \, \mathrm{d}V \\
\frac{\mathrm{d}n_i}{\mathrm{d}t} &= \int_0^V \nu_i r \, \mathrm{d}V \\
\frac{\mathrm{d}n_i}{\mathrm{d}t} &= \nu_i r V .
\end{aligned}
$$ (eq-batch-n)

We can work with concentrations $c_i = n_i/V$ to make this an intensive molar balance, which no
longer depends on the size of the system. As a second assumption we take the volume to be constant,
$V \neq f(t)$ — a stainless steel tank with a fixed liquid volume, say:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d}\left(c_i V\right)}{\mathrm{d}t} &= \nu_i r V \\
\frac{\mathrm{d}c_i}{\mathrm{d}t} &= \nu_i r ,
\end{aligned}
$$ (eq-batch-c)

or, for multiple reactions,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}c_i}{\mathrm{d}t} = \sum_j \nu_{ij} r_j .
$$ (eq-batch-c-multi)

This collection of component balances — one for each of the $S$ species, coupled through the $M$
reactions — is written compactly in matrix form as

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\vect{c}}{\mathrm{d}t} = \mtrx{N}\, \vect{r} ,
$$ (eq-batch-c-matrix)

where $\vect{c} = (c_1, \dots, c_S)^{\T}$ are the species concentrations,
$\vect{r} = (r_1, \dots, r_M)^{\T}$ are the reaction rates, and $\mtrx{N}$ is the $S \times M$
stoichiometric matrix with entries $(\mtrx{N})_{ij} = \nu_{ij}$. Each row of $\mtrx{N}$ holds the
stoichiometric coefficients of one species across all reactions; each column holds the coefficients
of one reaction across all species. For a system with the two reactions $\ce{A + B -> C}$ and
$\ce{2 A -> D}$, ordering the species as $(A, B, C, D)$ and the reactions as $(1, 2)$ gives

$$
\mtrx{N} = \begin{pmatrix}
-1 & -2 \\
-1 &  0 \\
 1 &  0 \\
 0 &  1
\end{pmatrix} .
$$

:::{admonition} Discussion
:class: seealso
What is the practical problem with using these balances to *measure* a reaction rate?
:::

Reaction rates cannot be measured directly; in practice we measure the time evolution of $c_i(t)$
and infer $r$ from $\mathrm{d}c_i/\mathrm{d}t$ via the balance.

If the volume changes during reaction — as it can in a gas-phase batch reactor whenever
$\sum_i \nu_i \neq 0$ — the constant-volume simplification no longer applies, and the concentration
balance picks up a volume-correction term:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}c_i}{\mathrm{d}t} = \nu_i r - c_i \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln V}{\mathrm{d}t} .
$$ (eq-batch-c-varv)

This is the mass balance for an **ideal** batch reactor: the contents are perfectly mixed, so
concentration and temperature are uniform throughout the reactor volume at every instant
($c_i \neq f(x,y,z)$, $T \neq f(x,y,z)$). Real batch reactors deviate from this idealization through
incomplete mixing and through temperature gradients driven by reaction enthalpy; in this course we
ignore both effects.

## Summary

- Systems are classified as *open* (mass and energy can cross the boundary), *closed* (only energy
  crosses), or *isolated* (neither crosses). Real chemical reactors are typically open or closed.
- The general balance, [](#eq-gen-balance) and [](#eq-gen-balance-rate), applies to mass, moles,
  atoms, momentum, and energy. The species mole balance [](#eq-mole-species),
  $\mathrm{d}n_i/\mathrm{d}t = \dot{n}_{i,0} - \dot{n}_i + \dot{p}_i$, is our workhorse.
- In a homogeneous reacting volume, the species production term is $\dot{p}_i = \nu_i r V$ for a
  single reaction, [](#eq-prod-homog), and $\dot{p}_i = V \sum_j \nu_{ij} r_j$ for $M$ simultaneous
  reactions, [](#eq-prod-homog-multi), with $r$ expressed in moles per volume per time.
- In a batch reactor there is no inlet or outlet: at constant volume the concentration balance
  reduces to $\mathrm{d}c_i/\mathrm{d}t = \nu_i r$, [](#eq-batch-c), or equivalently
  $\mathrm{d}\vect{c}/\mathrm{d}t = \mtrx{N}\,\vect{r}$ in matrix form,
  [](#eq-batch-c-matrix).
- When the reactor volume varies during reaction, the concentration balance gains a correction
  term, [](#eq-batch-c-varv).

These balances turn the design problem into a question about $r$: given the same vessel and feed,
the time evolution of $c_i$ is set entirely by the reaction rate. The next chapter develops the
rate laws that determine $r$, so that these balances become solvable for concrete reactions.
